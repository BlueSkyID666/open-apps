## What the MIT License lets you do

The MIT License is one of the shortest and most permissive open-source licences. It lets anyone use, copy, modify, merge, publish, distribute, sublicense and sell the software, in open or closed products, for free.

## What you must keep

One condition: keep the copyright notice and the licence text in all copies or substantial portions of the software. In practice that means shipping the original `LICENSE` file (or its text) with your product — in an "open-source notices" screen, a `NOTICE` file or your documentation.

The software comes "as is", without warranty; the authors are not liable for what happens when you use it.

## Can I use MIT code in a commercial, closed-source app?

Yes. The MIT License does not require you to publish your own source code or to license your changes under MIT. That is the main difference from copyleft licences such as [GPL-3.0](/licenses/gpl-3.0/) and [AGPL-3.0](/licenses/agpl-3.0/).

## MIT compared with other common licences

| | MIT | [Apache-2.0](/licenses/apache-2.0/) | [GPL-3.0](/licenses/gpl-3.0/) | [AGPL-3.0](/licenses/agpl-3.0/) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Use in closed-source products | Yes | Yes | Only if you do not distribute it | Only if you neither distribute it nor offer it over a network |
| Keep copyright and licence notice | Yes | Yes, plus NOTICE file | Yes | Yes |
| Explicit patent grant | No | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Must share your changes | No | No | When you distribute | When you distribute or serve it to users over a network |

## Things to check in the apps listed here

A repository's top-level licence is not always the whole story. Some MIT projects keep an enterprise directory under a separate commercial licence, bundle third-party assets with their own terms, or download AI models with separate licences. Each app's page on Open App Scout notes these splits under "Licence in practice".
